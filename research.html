  3. Peek-a-Book: Who Hides in the Limit Order Book, and When?" (FMA 2026 semifinalist in \textit{Market Microstructure})
    4. View Abstract
    This paper examines how uncertainty affects liquidity provision by incorporating macro, valuation, and execution uncertainties in a limit-order-book model. The model yields a spread decomposition and a hidden-share condition: hidden liquidity increases with macro uncertainty but decreases with valuation uncertainty because reduced time priority of hidden orders raises non-execution and selection costs. The macro effect is heterogeneous in equilibrium: when macro uncertainty is high, liquidity providers provide hidden liquidity to easier-to-value stocks and remove from harder-to-value stocks to minimize overall portfolio exposure. Consistent with these predictions, I show that increases in macro uncertainty raise hidden liquidity for easier-to-value firms and reduce it for harder-to-value firms. Robustness tests with alternative proxies and exogenous shocks from the COVID-19 pandemic and the temporary suspension of NYSE floor trading support a causal interpretation.
  4. The Disappearing Sensitivity of Stock Price Efficiency to Policy Uncertainty" (with M. Karim) (under review)
    5. View Abstract
    We document substantial instability in the relation between equity-market policy uncertainty and stock price efficiency. Examining all U.S. common stocks over 2001–2024 using a validated daily price-delay measure, benchmarked against Hasbrouck pricing errors through 2015, we find that uncertainty was associated with higher price delay, wider bid–ask spreads, and lower turnover during 2001–2009. A one-standard-deviation increase in uncertainty raised aggregate price delay by roughly nine percent of its mean. Structural break tests date the disappearance of the relation to October 2009. The post-break coefficient remains episodically unstable for several years before settling near zero by late 2016, and the null persists through the COVID-19 pandemic. The efficiency and liquidity responses to uncertainty change around the same break, while the uncertainty index remains informative about market-relevant uncertainty. These patterns are consistent with a transformation in liquidity provision, although the aggregate evidence does not identify that transformation as the unique cause of the break. The SEC Tick Size Pilot's randomized market-design restrictions reduce liquidity and slow price discovery but do not restore uncertainty sensitivity. The findings demonstrate that pooled-sample estimates can obscure economically distinct market regimes and caution against treating uncertainty–market-quality relations as structurally stable.
  5. Biodiversity Transition Risk and Asset Stranding in the Oil and Gas Industry (with M. Karim) (under review)
    6. View Abstract
    Research on the transition risk facing fossil-fuel producers has focused almost entirely on carbon. We study a distinct nature-related transition risk: exposure to biodiversity regulation, which threatens the land access and drilling rights on which oil and gas extraction depends. Using the frequency of biodiversity regulatory language in firms' pre-period 10-K filings as a predetermined exposure measure, we run a difference-in-differences design around the 2020 intensification of biodiversity governance. Oil and gas producers with greater pre-period exposure sharply reduce capital expenditure after 2020, by 6.9\% of lagged assets---roughly half the sector's pre-period investment rate. The reduction is a genuine contraction: research and development does not move and total investment falls commensurately, consistent with the stranding of undeveloped reserves. Identification rests on a within-industry dose-response design: firms that disclosed biodiversity regulation risk more persistently before 2020 cut investment monotonically more than their same-industry peers, absorbing the common 2020 demand and oil-price shocks. The gradient is firm-specific under randomization inference, survives oil-price and exploration controls and a post-COP15 treatment cutoff, and holds under a direct control for firm-level COVID-19 exposure. Nature-related regulation is becoming a material transition risk for oil and gas, distinct from carbon.

  7. Answering the Crowd: Corporate Disclosure Responses to Social-Media Attention
    8. View Abstract
    How do firms respond when the social-media crowd turns its attention to them? Using the cross-platform attention and sentiment signal of Cookson et al. (2024) merged with SEC filings for a 2012–2021 firm-day panel, we show that managers answer attention with disclosure. The probability of an 8-K filing rises significantly with the recent run-up in social-media attention—not sentiment—and the response concentrates in the discretionary filings managers control. These timed disclosures interact with a genuine over-reaction. Filings into high attention follow pronounced price run-ups and are followed by significant reversals within a week, while filings into low attention show no reversal. Probing the mechanism, the timed disclosures follow price moves in both directions, carry price-deflating announcement returns, and are not followed by insider sales or offerings—a pattern of corrective response to attention-driven mispricing rather than exploitation of it. Reading the text of all 25,703 voluntary filings corroborates the channel. Filings released into high attention are significantly more likely to contain explicitly corrective language, and corrective filings follow price declines and volatility. The disclosure results survive controls for earnings-announcement windows and, critically, for abnormal trading volume. Two comparison margins sharpen the interpretation. Insiders sell into attention for liquidity rather than timing (their attention-window sales precede smaller declines), and equity issuance is timed to unusual trading activity broadly, with no attention-specific component and no post-issuance return payoff. The crowd’s attention, we conclude, summons information from the firm—and the firm’s answer helps correct the very mispricing the attention creates.
  8. Inside a Structural Disagreement Measure: Composition, Validation, and Decay
    9. View Abstract
    Investor disagreement is unobservable and its proxies correlate weakly, so a recent literature builds model-implied composites whose internal composition is set by theory rather than by fit. That composition has never been examined, because the latent variable such a measure estimates was itself unobservable. This paper shows that the DIS measure of Goulding et al. (2026, JFE) is algebraically invertible: released under five risk-aversion settings, its two structural blocks and the latent belief-precision term solve exactly, and three restrictions the mapping imposes hold against I/B/E/S, Compustat and CRSP data the recovery never uses, at correlations above 0.91. The composition is lopsided. One block carries 98.6% of the variance, and the analyst inputs---the only ones derived from what an identifiable population says about a specific quantity---supply 41% of belief precision in the lowest-disagreement decile but 13% in the highest, leaving the measure nearly inactive as a measure of beliefs precisely where it earns its return predictability. Those returns are themselves fragile. The 21.5% annualized alpha falls to 4 to $7% against realized volatility, and that residual is confined to 1994--2008: over 2009--2022 idiosyncratic volatility spans the composite rather than the reverse, while every compositional finding replicates in both halves.
